7.17 National Gallery of Art, Washington: A World of Art by Martha Richler (Scala Books, 1998) [anti-misattribution]#

National Gallery of Art, Washington: A World of Art (Scala Books / Philip Wilson Publishers, London, 1998) is sometimes attributed in part to Tartt, but she made no contribution to it — no foreword, introduction, preface, quoted passage, or bio mention. Full-text examination (assets/sources/archive/NGA-Scala-Richler-1999-9781857591767-AnnasArchive.pdf) returns zero matches for "tartt"; "donna" matches only "Madonna and Child" (paintings). The Amazon UK author-list attribution that surfaced this candidate is a metadata-aggregator error; Open Library, Internet Archive, and LOC records all credit Richler alone.

Bibliographic record (corrected):

  • Title: National Gallery of Art, Washington: A World of Art
  • Author: Martha Richler (sole author)
  • Editor: Cherry Lewis
  • Designer: Gillian Greenwood
  • Publisher: Scala Books, an imprint of Philip Wilson Publishers Limited, London
  • US/Canada distribution: Antique Collectors' Club Limited, Wappingers' Falls, NY
  • Publication date: first published 1998
  • Copyright: © 1997 Text/design by Philip Wilson Publishers; © 1997 illustrations National Gallery of Art
  • ISBN-13: 978-1-85759-176-3 (HC), 978-1-85759-187-3 (PB)
  • LCCN: 97-061688
  • Pages: ~224 (introduction begins p. 8 with Mellon-Roosevelt history; eleven thematic chapters)
  • The book's "Introduction" beginning at p. 8 is by Richler herself (not by any external contributor) and concerns Andrew W. Mellon's December 1936 letter to Roosevelt offering his collection.

Consequence for chronology: The earliest confirmed Tartt foreword/introduction is §7.1 (Charles Portis, True Grit, Bloomsbury UK reissue, January 17, 2005). Life of St Francis (§7.23, February 1, 2005) follows by 15 days as second-earliest.

Tartt's contribution — content: An autobiographical memoir-essay. Tartt frames her literary formation through her great-grandmother's reading-aloud sessions in Mississippi. Key biographical and bibliographic content:

  • The great-grandmother — born Mississippi 1890, lived in Mississippi for her whole life, but with Scottish ancestry whose lullaby-voice (which Tartt only later recognized as a Scots accent) shaped Tartt's early sensibility.
  • Books they read together — "good books, bad books, strange old-fashioned books from her own childhood … Billy Whiskers, Little Prudy, Peck's Bad Boy"; plus Chitty-Chitty Bang-Bang; and True Grit by Charles Portis. This documents that Tartt read True Grit in early childhood, in reading-aloud sessions with her great-grandmother, providing direct biographical context for her 2005 Bloomsbury True Grit introduction (§7.1) issued the same year as this Fairy Tale Review essay.
  • Peter Pan — the central preoccupation: Tartt argues Peter Pan is "ultimately such a dark book, about change, loss, aging, mortality, death." At her great-grandmother's funeral Tartt (as a small child) silently prayed to Peter rather than to a Christian God. She suggests Peter is "much much older, indeed a god in his own right," noting Hook calls him "by his true name: Pan."
  • Stevenson — Tartt treats Stevenson as the more important of the two for her own development: "Treasure Island and Kidnapped were the real bridge for me between the child's world and the adult's. Essentially, they were the books that turned me into a novelist." She quotes Stevenson on illustrating "any object mentioned in the text, were it only a loaf of bread or a candlestick."
  • Little Friend disclosure: "The whole time I was writing my second novel, The Little Friend, I had a picture of Stevenson on the wall by my desk. It's a story he would have liked, I think: a clever, bookish child pitted against a gang of criminals. And it's probably not a story it would have occurred to me to tell, if not for him." A Tartt-authored statement of Stevenson as the primary literary model behind The Little Friend. Useful for §3 (TLF) cross-reference.
  • Closing line: "She never saw Scotland herself—but even so, when I was just tiny, she made sure to press Alan Breck's silver button tight into my hand, and fold my fingers around it. And I am holding onto it still." (Alan Breck Stewart is the protagonist of Kidnapped.)
  • The essay closes with a self-disclosed upcoming Edinburgh trip — Tartt traveling to Edinburgh "in a few weeks" (i.e., spring 2005, contemporaneous with the Bloomsbury True Grit publication January 17, 2005 and the Life of St. Francis foreword February 1, 2005).

Major biographical and critical content extracted from the essay:

  • Tartt's great-grandmother born in Mississippi, 1890, lived in Mississippi her whole long life. Her grandparents (who died before Tartt was born) were Scottish — vestiges of Scottishness survived in her nursery talk. Tartt's great-grandfather was generations removed from his French ancestors. The "private lullaby voice" Tartt thought was her great-grandmother's alone turned out to be a Scots accent (revelation as a child via TV).
  • Reading sessions: 8 hours at a stretch, sometimes whole books in a day. Read together in the great-grandmother's bedroom. Their houses were "only a few houses apart" — they would call and wave at each other on the corner of Levee Street before turning the corner.
  • Books read with great-grandmother: Billy Whiskers, Little Prudy, Peck's Bad Boy, Chitty-Chitty Bang-Bang, True Grit by Charles Portis. (This pre-dates Tartt's 2005 introduction to the Bloomsbury reissue of True Grit — §7.1 — by decades.)
  • Modern books were ordered in hardcover by Tartt's great Aunt Frances, head librarian at the town library.
  • Peter Pan — read repeatedly "almost as if it were a religious text"; at her great-grandmother's funeral, the child Tartt prayed to Peter rather than to a heavenly God (described as "fierce sunburnt little pagan").
  • Treasure Island — first encountered at age 8 or 9; "hopelessly under the spell." Then Kidnapped, "even more captivated."
  • Stevenson's poems from A Child's Garden of Verses — Tartt's great-grandmother knew several by heart.
  • The Little Friend composition: Tartt had a picture of Stevenson on the wall by her desk while writing The Little Friend. "It's a story he would have liked, I think: a clever, bookish child pitted against a gang of criminals. And it's probably not a story it would have occurred to me to tell, if not for him."
  • Edinburgh trip: "I'm visiting Edinburgh in a few weeks" — dating Tartt's essay to circa late 2004 / early 2005. "She made sure to press Alan Breck's silver button tight into my hand, and fold my fingers around it. And I am holding onto it still."
  • Cited references (with footnotes): Hennessy, James Pope, Robert Louis Stevenson (Simon and Schuster, 1974), p. 19; Sidney Colvin (ed.), The Letters of Robert Louis Stevenson, Vol. 1 (Methuen, 1900), p. 258; Treasure Island (Puffin Books, 1994) at pp. 27, 40, 66.

Author bio (in contributor notes): "DONNA TARTT is the author of the novels The Secret History and The Little Friend." (Absent of The Goldfinch — confirms 2005 contributor-bio context.)

Tartt's essay appears in the Blue Issue between Kiki Smith ("Six Prints") and Sara Veglahn ("Two Poems").

The four Rimbaud poems Tartt translated:

  1. "Bottom" — French original ("La réalité étant trop épineuse pour mon grand caractère...") + Tartt's English translation ("Reality being entirely too spiny and thorny for my grand character...").
  2. "Being Beauteous" ("Devant une neige un Etre de Beauté de haute taille...") + Tartt's translation ("An Ideal of Beauty, standing tall before snow...").
  3. "Vies" ("Lives") — three numbered sections (I, II, III). Section I begins "O les énormes avenues du pays saint..." / "O the enormous avenues of the Holy land...". Section III closes "Je suis réellement d'outre-tombe, et pas de commissions." / "I really am from beyond the grave, and without obligations."
  4. "Phrases" — multiple aphoristic prose-poem fragments. Begins "Quand le monde sera réduit en un seul bois noir pour nos quatre yeux étonnés..." / "Some day, when the world boils down to a single black wood for our four astonished eyes..."

Source-text edition: Rimbaud's Les Illuminations (composed c. 1872–1875; first published posthumously 1886 in La Vogue). Tartt presents her translations en face — French original on one page, English translation on the facing page.

Editorial framing (Carmen Giminez Smith, Green Issue editor's note on Rimbaud): Brief biographical note on Rimbaud ("born in 1854, in a small town called Charleville in the Ardennes... began writing poetry at age thirteen... by age 19 had completed The Drunken Boat, A Season in Hell and The Illuminations... gave up writing forever; he went to Africa, where he worked at exporting coffee and running guns; and was dead by the age of 37"). Quotes Rimbaud from Une Saison en Enfer: Délires II on his love of "dumb paintings, scenes painted in door panels, stage sets, painted canvas backdrops for acrobats, flags and standards, popular colored prints, old novels gone out of fashion, church Latin, erotic books with bad spelling, the novels of our grandmothers, fairy tales, little books from childhood, foolish refrains and naive rhythms."

Author bio (in contributor notes): "DONNA TARTT is the author of the novels The Secret History and The Little Friend." (Same as Blue Issue 2005 — confirms pre-Goldfinch context.)

The selection (Bottom, Being Beauteous, Vies, Phrases) draws from across Les Illuminations and is consistent with Tartt's documented French-language reading (cf. her Camus dinner-date answer in §7.16; her time in France carrying The Unquiet Grave by Cyril Connolly). This is the first and only documented Tartt translation programme of any text by another author.

  1. Theological/literary framing of Francis (pp. v–vi). Tartt sets up Francis as "the greatest ambassador of the Church—the ideal introduction to Christianity for people of different faiths and cultures." She characterizes his message as "a faith so radiantly inclusive that it hails the birds as sisters, and addresses a cricket with as much reverence and courtesy as a bishop." Cites G.K.   2. Bonaventure's craft (p. vii). Tartt explains why Bonaventure (canonized only two years after Francis's death) wrote this life: "Bonaventure's anecdotes give a sharp sense of the living man himself. Francis is whimsical and affectionate and almost always in a hurry... he is at once excessively polite and bewilderingly abrupt; he is often very funny; he is generous almost to lunacy."

Discloses that she "first became intrigued by St. Francis of Assisi when I was about fourteen years old, thanks to Zooey Glass's remark to Franny, in Franny and Zooey, that St. Francis was more loveable than Jesus." (Perhaps other children were edified to learn that working mothers were destined for hell fire, or by being forced to wear black yarn tied around a finger signifying that they were 'against Christ,' but I was not one of them)."

Cross-religion comparisons: Tartt characterizes Francis as "very reminiscent of some of the great Buddhist saints and Boddhisatvas" and "probably the closest thing we have to a bhakti or a sufi in the Western tradition." Concludes with G.K. Chesterton's framing of Francis as "the court fool of the King of Paradise."

Significance: The foreword contains Tartt's most explicit published criticism of the fundamentalist religious culture in which she was raised. The "black yarn tied around a finger signifying they were 'against Christ'" reference is a vivid first-person datum about her PDS / Greenwood-Mississippi adolescence and a useful primary source for any biographical treatment of Tartt's religious background and its influence on her fiction (cf. the religious-mystic strand of The Little Friend and The Goldfinch's closing meditation).

Tartt's piece — full opening: "Not long ago, my little godson came to stay with me for the first time: his first summer vacation, and also his first trip to the countryside. Though still an infant, not yet able to speak, his eyes were round and ringing with astonishment all weekend long. The hummingbird memory (core of the piece): "The very earliest memory of my own life, and will be with me until I die, is of the first time I saw a hummingbird. The incident was inconsequential enough; I was about four years old, and had accompanied my beloved great-grandmother (then in her late seventies) to a garden party given for a distant relative: a young bride-to-be. It was springtime: the azaleas were in spectacular bloom; the astonishing little ruby-throated creature flew right in front of me — down at my eye level, practically in front of my face — and hovered there for some moments before it buzzed forward, then backward, then flew away across the green lawn for ever."

Tartt's mid-life reflection (closing meditation): "Only now — at mid-life, in my fortieth year — am I starting to realize what the hummingbird means, and why — at unexpected moments — it returns to me still. It is a premonition of Heaven, and of Death. My great-grandmother (who was leaning beside me, holding my hand, as the hummingbird paused in mid-air before me) did not have long to live. Nor did the bride herself — lovely laughing Ginger, who died young, of cancer."

Dating the piece: Tartt born December 23, 1963. "In my fortieth year" = 2003–2004 (her 40th birthday was Dec 23, 2003). Bloomsbury volume was published 2004. Tartt wrote this piece specifically for the Burningham anthology, in 2003–2004 — not a reprint, an original commission.

  • "Elderly pug (bigger than he was, a frightening but friendly lion)" — the Tartt pug Pongo (born ~1987, see USA Today 2002 interview O09).

7.28 Tartt essay "On True Grit" reprinted — Escape Velocity: A Charles Portis Miscellany, ed. Jay Jennings (Butler Center Books / Overlook Press, 2012–2013); revised edition with new Tartt afterword (University of Arkansas Press, 2021)#

The 2012/2013 first edition reprints Tartt's 2005 True Grit introduction (§7.1) in the Appendix: Tributes. The 2021 revised edition adds a second, distinct Tartt text — a new afterword adapted from her 2020 NYT Portis tribute (§6.6). Two separate Tartt appearances across two editions of the same volume.

Tartt contribution — First edition / Overlook reissue:

Title as printed: "On True Grit" (chapter heading). Positioned in: Appendix: Tributes — the final section of the book, after the main Portis texts, the epilogue interview, and before the Sources and Acknowledgements.

Nature of text: This is a reprint of Tartt's introduction to the Bloomsbury 2005 UK reissue of True Grit (§7.1). The acknowledgements page confirms: "The authors of the appreciations in the appendix — Roy Blount Jr., Ed Park, Ron Rosenbaum, Donna Tartt, and Wells Tower — get great appreciation in turn from me for their eagerness to allow their work to be reprinted here." The text is identical to the essay confirmed via the Overlook 2010 True Grit EPUB (§7.1, ~2,915 words); EPUB extraction from the Overlook 2013 Escape Velocity EPUB yields ~2,924 words — negligibly different, consistent with the same text across two slightly different digital transcriptions.

Contributor bio note as printed: "Donna Tartt is the author of the novels The Secret History (1992) and The Little Friend (2002)." — Pre-Goldfinch (2013) wording, consistent with the essay having been commissioned in 2004–2005 for the Bloomsbury reissue. No update was made to the bio note for the 2013 Overlook reissue.

Structural position in the Appendix: Tartt's piece is the fourth of five tributes, between Ron Rosenbaum's "Our Least-Known Great Novelist" and Wells Tower's "The Book That Changed My Life: Gringos." Full tribute sequence:

  1. Roy Blount Jr. — "Comedy in Earnest"
  2. Ed Park — "Like Cormac McCarthy, but Funny"
  3. Ron Rosenbaum — "Our Least-Known Great Novelist"
  4. Donna Tartt — "On True Grit"
  5. Wells Tower — "The Book That Changed My Life: Gringos"

EPUB confirmation: Full text confirmed via EPUB at assets/sources/archive/EscapeVelocity-Portis-OverlookPress-2013-9781468308495-AnnaArchive.epub. Tartt essay at OEBPS/Text/chapter022.html. Text opens: "It's commonplace to say that we 'love' a book, but when we say it, we really mean all sorts of things…" Closes: "Now — thankfully — the book is back in print, and I am delighted to have the honor of introducing Mattie Ross and Rooster Cogburn to a new generation of readers." Full text identical to §7.1 canonical text confirmed via Brick Magazine (brickmag.com/the-great-abiding-pleasure-of-true-grit) and the Overlook 2010 True Grit EPUB.

Tartt contribution — Revised edition (University of Arkansas Press, 2021):

Title as printed: Afterword (by Donna Tartt). Nature of text: A new, distinct Tartt text — not the 2005 True Grit introduction. Per the UA Press product page: "This revised edition includes a new afterword by best-selling author Donna Tartt, who first published her remembrance in the New York Times following Portis's death in 2020." This afterword is an adaptation of §6.6 (Tartt, "Donna Tartt on the Singular Voice, and Pungent Humor, of Charles Portis," NYT Book Review, June 9, 2020; print edition June 28, 2020 retitled "True Grit"). Whether the 2021 afterword is identical to the 2020 NYT text or a revised/expanded version is not established.

The 2021 afterword is not in the Overlook 2013 EPUB. Collectors seeking the 2021 Tartt text must acquire the UA Press revised edition (ISBN 978-1-945624-26-1).

Collecting significance:

  • The first edition (Butler Center Books, 2012, ISBN 978-1-935106-50-0) is the first print publication of this Tartt text in an American hardcover anthology — the Bloomsbury 2005 UK introduction had not previously appeared in a US hardcover context.
  • The Overlook 2013 paperback is more readily found on the secondary market and documents the same Tartt essay text.
  • The 2021 UA Press revised edition is the only print venue for Tartt's second Portis essay text (the 2020 NYT tribute adapted as afterword) — a distinct collecting target from the 2012/2013 editions.
  • The Overlook 2013 ad-card documents Tartt's afterword across multiple Overlook True Grit sub-editions: "It is available from Overlook in a movie tie-in edition and a mass market edition, both of which include an afterword by Donna Tartt, as well as a young readers edition, with an afterword by Leonard S. Marcus." This confirms the Tartt True Grit essay circulated in at minimum three distinct Overlook True Grit formats (movie tie-in, mass market, and the 2010 reprint EPUB at §7.1) in addition to the Bloomsbury UK 2005 first appearance.
