7.1 Introduction: Charles Portis, True Grit (Bloomsbury reissue, 2005)#

Covers:

  • CON-001 — True Grit (Bloomsbury 2005) - Tartt introduction: assets/images/_retrieved/CON-001-truegrit-bloomsbury.jpg
  • CON-001b — True Grit (Overlook 2010 reissue): assets/images/_retrieved/CON-001b-publisher.jpg

Tartt wrote a substantive introduction to Bloomsbury's 2005 UK reissue (published January 17, 2005). The same essay was carried forward in the Overlook Press (US) 2010 movie tie-in edition (ISBN 9781590204597) as an afterword. [86]

Guardian pre-publication excerpt (January 8, 2005): An edited version of this introduction was published in The Guardian nine days before the Bloomsbury reissue, under the title "How Mattie got her man" (The Guardian, Saturday, January 8, 2005, pages G6-03 and G7-03). Section: "6 Lives & letters / Rereadings." Full headline: "Donna Tartt on True Grit, a modern classic of one girl's quest to avenge her father's murder / How Mattie got her man." Pull quote: "No living Southern writer captures the spoken idioms of the South as Portis does." The Guardian's own footnote on p. G7-03 reads: "This is an edited version of Donna Tartt's introduction to a new edition of True Grit, published by Bloomsbury on January 17. To order a copy please see the details of the Guardian book service on page 20." Primary scans: assets/articles/Guardian_2005-01-08_HowMattieGotHerMan_G6-03.pdf and assets/articles/Guardian_2005-01-08_HowMattieGotHerMan_G7-03.pdf.

The Guardian, Saturday January 8, 2005 is the earliest print publication of this essay text (as an edited excerpt), predating the Bloomsbury reissue by nine days. The full, unedited introduction appears in the Bloomsbury book (pub. January 17, 2005) and the Overlook Press (US) 2010 movie tie-in (ISBN 9781590204597).

Canonical URL (full introduction text): brickmag.com/the-great-abiding-pleasure-of-true-grit (Brick Magazine #76, 2005 — full introduction text, freely archived; title in Brick: "The Great, Abiding Pleasure of True Grit"). Guardian excerpt ("How Mattie got her man," Jan 8, 2005): theguardian.com/books/2005/jan/08/classics.charlieportis (held in the Newspapers.com archive).

Text status: Full introduction — FREE (Brick Magazine online archive). Guardian excerpt (edited version) — not accessible online; primary newspaper scans held locally.

Overlook 2010 reissue: Full text of the essay collates from assets/sources/archive/TrueGrit-Portis-OverlookPress-2010Reprint-9781585673698-AnnasArchive.epub. In the Overlook edition the essay is positioned as the AFTERWORD (TOC heading: "AFTERWORD"; opening byline: "DONNA TARTT"; placed after the seven novel sections, consistent with the §7.1 note that Bloomsbury's "introduction" was carried forward by Overlook as an "afterword"). Length: ~2,915 words. The Bloomsbury 2005 introduction text and the Overlook 2010 afterword text are the same essay in two structural positions across two imprints.

Structural outline of Tartt's essay (six discernible movements within the ~2,915 words):

  1. What it means to "love" a book. Opens with the distinction between books one read once long ago, books one knows by hearsay, and the smaller class of books one returns to repeatedly across a lifetime. Argues True Grit belongs to the third category and is therefore a masterpiece.
  2. First encounters with the novel. Discusses her family's relationship with the book — her mother kept losing and replacing copies because friends never returned them; in one episode her mother borrowed the library's copy and pretended to have lost it. Recounts reading True Grit as part of Honors English alongside Whitman, Hawthorne, and Poe at her Mississippi school.
  3. Mattie Ross as narrator. Detailed close reading of Mattie's voice — its blend of biblical formality, Presbyterian moralism, frontier vernacular, and quiet humor. Treats Mattie as a unique American narrative voice rivalling Huck Finn.
  4. Rooster Cogburn / Marshal Cogburn portrait. The novel's transformative achievement, per Tartt, is to take a stock figure (the drunken one-eyed federal marshal) and make him morally complex; argues the John Wayne film flattens this.
  5. Southern/Western boundary location. True Grit's geographical-temporal setting is "where chivalry meets the frontier — where the old Confederacy starts to merge and shade away into the Wild West." The book closes at a travelling Wild West show in early-1900s Memphis: "at once in the twentieth century and firmly enshrined in myth and legend."
  6. Publication history and rediscovery. Cites Roald Dahl's 1968 review ("the best novel to come my way in a long time… Since what? What book has given me greater pleasure in the last five years? Or in the last twenty?"). Closes with the rationale for the reissue and the Tartt-as-evangelist framing for introducing the novel to a new generation of readers.

Key Tartt-authored line (canonical pull-quote, also used by Guardian as their headline): "No living Southern writer captures the spoken idioms of the South as Portis does."

Other notable passages:

  • "I think it goes without saying that most books that engage readers on this very high level are masterpieces; and this is why I believe that True Grit by Charles Portis is a masterpiece." (opening framing)
  • The mother-borrowing-the-library-copy anecdote is a memorable autobiographical disclosure; useful for §3-cross-reference to Tartt's biographical-Mississippi material
  • The Roald Dahl quotation is a useful secondary citation for any TG reception history

Cross-references:

  • The Fairy Tale Review Blue Issue 2005 essay (§7.19, "On Barrie and Stevenson") independently confirms Tartt's lifelong relationship with the book — her great-grandmother had read True Grit aloud to her in early childhood, which provides the deep biographical context for this 2005 introduction
  • The 2020 NYTBR Portis tribute (§6.11) revisits many of these themes after Portis's death in February 2020 — usefully read as the bookend to the 2005 essay
  • The Overlook 2010 reissue (ISBN 9781585673698) appears to share the standardized Overlook editioning ISBN with the 2010 movie tie-in (ISBN 9781590204597 noted earlier in this entry)

7.5 Tartt poem “True Crime” in Otto Penzler ed., Murder For Love (Delacorte Press, 1996)#

Covers:

  • CON-004 — Murder For Love - Penzler ed (Delacorte 1996): assets/images/_retrieved/CON-004-publisher.jpg
  • CON-004b — Murder For Love - Dell paperback: assets/images/_retrieved/CON-004b-cover-openlibrary.jpg

Tartt contributed a poem titled “True Crime” to Murder For Love, an anthology edited by Otto Penzler and published by Delacorte Press, January/February 1996. The trade hardcover ISBN is 0-385-31466-3 / 9780385314664 (the Delacorte hardcover). The Dell mass-market reissue ISBN is 0440613582 / 9780440613589. Additional US paperback (Random House, March 1999): ISBN 0440221056. UK editions: UK first edition hardback (Orion Publishing Co): ISBN 9780752807355 / 0752807358 (November 18, 1996; 329pp). UK Orion paperback: ISBN 9780752808901 / 0752808907 (London: Orion, 1996; 323pp; OCLC 438383727; OL18231702M). Later UK editions: ISBN 9780752811208 (Severn House) and 9780708989838 (Charnwood large print). [31]

Bibliographic detail:

Anthology format: 16 selections – fifteen short stories plus one poem (Tartt's). Penzler editor's introduction frames the volume as “all related to love (usually misguided), with page-turning suspense, humor, and action.” Confirmed contributors and works:

  • William J. Caunitz – “Dying Time”
  • Carol Higgins Clark – “For Whom the Beep Tolls”
  • Mary Higgins Clark – “Definitely, a Crime of Passion”
  • James Crumley – “Hot Springs” (Crumley's first short story in 23 years per editor's note)
  • John Gardner – “The Loving You Get”
  • Faye Kellerman – “The Stalker”
  • Jonathan Kellerman – “The Things We Do for Love”
  • Elmore Leonard – “Karen Makes Out” (Leonard's first short story in 30+ years per editor's note)
  • Michael Malone – “Red Clay”
  • Bobbie Ann Mason – “Nancy Drew Remembers (A Parody)”
  • Ed McBain – “Running from Legs”
  • Joyce Carol Oates – “At the Paradise Motel, Sparks, Nevada”
  • Sara Paretsky – “Heartbreak House”
  • Anne Perry – “The Blackmailer”
  • Shel Silverstein – “For What She Had Done”
  • Donna Tartt – “True Crime” (poem; the only verse contribution in the volume)

Critical/dealer description: Between the Covers ABAA describes the Tartt poem as “beautifully enigmatic and rife with what is not said” and notes the collection sits “in the school of American noir.” The Donna Tartt Shrine maintains a dedicated page for the poem at languageisavirus.com/donna-tartt/poem-true-crime.php (the page reproduces editorial context; Tartt has not authorised reproduction of “True Crime” outside the Penzler volume).

Provenance link to TSH 1992: a Captain Ahab's Rare Books TSH first inscribed by Tartt to Otto Penzler dated 9/10/92 [Source: S088] [106] (six days pre-publication of TSH on September 16, 1992) documents the Tartt-Penzler personal connection that preceded the 1996 anthology placement by approximately three and a half years.

Subsequent reprints of “True Crime”: no anthology reprint of the Tartt poem has been traced through ABAA / AbeBooks / Goodreads / FictionDB infrastructure. The poem appears unique to the 1996 Penzler anthology. No “Best American Poetry” inclusion or other anthology placement has surfaced.

Full text of the “True Crime” poem is reproduced at languageisavirus.com — languageisavirus.com/donna-tartt/poem-true-crime.php. Poem text: 13-line vignette in prose-poem form (“Things were getting hot in Idaho…”). The Murder For Love TOC (as reproduced on languageisavirus.com) classifies Tartt’s contribution as “True Crime by Donna Tartt (vignette, reprint).” The text reflects the original publication (Donna Tartt Shrine, a third-party fan site).

Canonical URL (reference page with poem text): languageisavirus.com/donna-tartt/poem-true-crime.php (Donna Tartt Shrine — full poem text; documents multiple ISBN variants for the anthology).

Text status: POEM TEXT: FREE at languageisavirus.com/donna-tartt/poem-true-crime.php. ANTHOLOGY: Print only (Delacorte hardcover 1996; Dell paperback; Mysterious Press UK; audiobook edition). Audio edition: "Murder for Love: Murder for Women" (Dove Audio, 6 Hours, January 1996) — "True Crime" labeled "(poem, reprint)" in audio TOC.

Anthology in library: The full Dell paperback EPUB (ISBN 9780440221050, Bantam Doubleday Dell, 1996/1999 reissue) is archived at assets/sources/archive/auction_records/Murder for Love-...AnnasArchive.epub. Penzler's editorial intro to the Tartt section and the poem itself are extracted to assets/sources/archive/excerpts/Tartt-TrueCrime-MurderForLove-Penzler-1996-poem-p323.html.

Page position: Tartt's section begins at p. 323 of the Dell paperback (per the volume's own TOC line: "TRUE CRIME by Donna Tartt … 323"). The Penzler editorial introduction precedes the poem; the poem itself runs ~13 lines as the closing piece of Penzler's "True Crime" chapter. The next anthology piece is William J. Caunitz's "Dying Time" (begins p. ~328).

Penzler's editorial framing: Penzler explicitly notes the volume's "inflexible rule was that all stories had to be original, written especially for this book. We winked at the rule, as this is a poem. It had previously been read by about eleven subscribers to the Oxford Review." This establishes:

  • "True Crime" is not original to Murder For Love
  • Penzler's "Oxford Review" is a paraphrase/error — the actual prior publication venue is The Oxford American (see "Original venue" block below)
  • The poem predates Murder For Love (1996) by approximately two years

Original venue: The Delacorte 1996 hardcover copyright page and Oxford American's own archival TOC for Issue 4 (Winter 1994), archived locally at assets/sources/archive/OA-Issue4-Winter1994-TOC-userArchive-20260527.webarchive (paired extracted HTML at OA-Issue4-Winter1994-TOC-userArchive-20260527.html):

  • The Delacorte 1996 copyright page carries an explicit individual-story credit line: "'True Crime' by Donna Tartt Copyright © 1996 by Donna Tartt. First appeared in *The Oxford America[n]."* (the copyright page has a minor typesetting drop reading "Oxford America" without the final "n," but the magazine is unmistakably The Oxford American).
  • Oxford American Issue 4, Winter 1994 — full TOC from the archived webarchive: COLUMNS & DEPARTMENTS by Will D. Campbell, Clyde Edgerton, Chris Offutt. FEATURES include Photographs ("Racing on Mississippi Dirt" by Susan Lee), four Commentary pieces (Florence King on Scarlett; Steve Vineberg on Tennessee Williams; Jeff Baker; John Hester on Bosnia), Reportage by Scott Morris (24-Hour Elvis), Review by Charles Taylor. FICTION by Madison Jones and Daniel Lyons. ESSAY by Gerald Wade ("Sieging Vicksburg"). POETRY: Azza, by Jamie Simpson — On the Banks of the University, by Charles Bukowski — Flood, by J.E. Pitts — True Crime, by Donna Tartt — The Rhino in the Burger King, by Ron Rash. Cover: Photography by William Eggleston.
  • Tartt-Eggleston pre-history: Issue 4's cover is by William Eggleston himself — the same photographer Tartt would later introduce in Artforum International Vol. 40 No. 2, October 2001 ("PORTFOLIO: William Eggleston," §7.12). The Eggleston-cover/Tartt-poem co-appearance in OA Issue 4 (Winter 1994) predates the Artforum portfolio by approximately seven years and establishes that the Tartt-Eggleston creative relationship was already in motion at OA in the early 1990s.
  • This resolves the §7.5 disambiguation question (Oxford American Review vs. Oxford American vs. UK Oxford Review). Penzler's "eleven subscribers" remark in Murder For Love was characteristically tongue-in-cheek about OA's then-modest circulation rather than literal.
  • Oxford American Issue 4 (Winter 1994) is the original-venue collecting target for the poem; back issues from this era are scarce. The Eggleston cover gives this issue a double-collector appeal (Tartt + Eggleston).
  • The Oxford American Tartt corpus comprises seven distinct pieces across 1992–2001: §6.1 (Basketball Season, Issue 2 Fall 1992), §6.8 (Spanish Grandeur in Mississippi, Issue 41 Fall 2001), §6.9 (In Melbourne, Issue 6 Mar/Apr 1995), §6.12 (Willie Morris tribute, Issue 29 Sep/Oct 1999), §6.3 (Spirituality in the Modern Novel, Issue 30 Nov/Dec 1999), §6.14 (Murder & Imagination, Issue 11 Spring 1996), and this "True Crime" appearance (Issue 4 Winter 1994).

7.7 Bomb Magazine “Editor’s Choice” (Summer 1996)#

Cover: assets/images/_retrieved/CON-006-bomb.jpg — CON-006 (Bomb Magazine Summer 1996 - Editor's Choice)

Tartt contributed an “Editor’s Choice” short essay to Bomb Magazine issue #56 (Summer 1996; cover: Martha Plimpton; “Famous for Fifteen Years Anniversary Issue”), writing about American contemporary painter Erin Parish. Bomb Magazine (Brooklyn, NY) is a respected quarterly arts journal covering contemporary art, music, literature, and film; its “Editor’s Choice” is a regular feature where notable cultural figures recommend an artist. Issue #56 also carries “Editor’s Choice” pieces by Lynn Geller (on Steve Earle & Iggy Pop), Jessica Hagedorn (on Lois-Ann Yamanaka & R.Z. Linmark), and Marvin Heiferman (on Per Maning). Tartt’s piece opens: “Erin Parish, daughter of artists Tom and Susan Parish, made her first oil painting when she was five… Now, in her late twenties, her painting still trembles with menace.” The work illustrated in the article is Parish’s Tapping for Maple Syrup (1996, oil on resin, 7¾ × 5½ × ¾ inches). Full text archived at: bombmagazine.org/articles/1996/07/01/erin-parish Citation: Bomb Magazine #56, Summer 1996, “Editor’s Choice: Erin Parish,” by Donna Tartt. The issue #56 TOC confirms the Tartt byline. The issue retailed for $4.50 cover price. [91]

Canonical URL: bombmagazine.org/articles/1996/07/01/erin-parish

Text status: FREE — full text accessible without registration (Bomb Magazine online archive).

7.8 Exhibition catalog: Erin Parish, “Into the Blue Again” (Jeffrey Coploff Fine Art, New York, 1997)#

Tartt wrote the text (essay) for the exhibition catalog accompanying Erin Parish’s solo show “Into the Blue Again” at Jeffrey Coploff Fine Art gallery, New York, 1997. Erin Parish is an American contemporary painter known for figurative and abstract work. Jeffrey Coploff Fine Art was an established New York gallery in the 1990s (incorporated 05/16/1995; 115 Fourth Avenue, Suite 4J, New York, NY 10003; NY DOS ID 1922676). [Source: S084]. Citation: “Into the Blue Again.” New York: Jeffrey Coploff, Donna Tartt, 1997. Print. Contemporary review: Review Magazine, “Fresh Perspectives at Jeffrey Coploff,” Annie Herron, 15 Mar. 1997. Print. Subsequent coverage: Art in America, “Review of Into the Blue Again,” Turner Grady, Oct. 1998, pp. 140–41. Print. Source: Erin Parish artist press record, artsalesandresearch.com; Mullenbooks dealer listing (viaLibri). Donna Tartt also published an essay on Erin Parish’s painting in BOMB Magazine #56, Summer 1996 — one year before the Jeffrey Coploff catalog. The essay (“Erin Parish” by Donna Tartt, BOMB 56, July 1, 1996) describes Parish’s work and her exhibition “Waterline at Black and Herron Gallery” (May 1996), establishing the Tartt-Parish creative relationship in a major arts publication prior to the 1997 catalog. [Source: S083]. The gallery is confirmed to have shown Parish’s work in its records (artist-info.com exhibition history). [92]

Physical examination: An examined copy collates as a softcover saddle-stitched square-format pamphlet; deep-blue underwater photographic field on the front cover with title reversed out in white caps; title-page interior reproduces the cover composition in black on white plus exhibition close-date notice ("The exhibition runs through January 10th at Jeffrey Coploff Fine Art LTD, 526 West 26th Street, 2nd Floor. Please stop by."); Tartt's essay occupies an interior spread; back panel carries a Herman Melville Moby Dick epigraph ("…AS EVERYONE KNOWS, MEDITATION AND WATER ARE WEDDED FOREVER.") and a credits block. Publication details: Copyright © 1997 Erin Parish; Design by Robert Gilson, San Francisco; Photography by Paul Waldman, New York; Scanning by ColorCopy, Burlingame, CA; Printing by Oscar Printing, San Francisco, CA. Tartt's essay is signed and dated "Donna Tartt, New York City, 4 October 1997" at the foot of the essay page — establishing 4 October 1997 as the composition date; the exhibition itself closed January 10, 1998 (so the show was on view from late 1997 into early 1998). The essay opens with a Greek-and-translation Heraclitus epigraph ("Ὁδὸς ἄνω κάτω μία καὶ ὡυτή — 'The way up and the way down are the same.'"). Print run unknown (typical gallery-catalog ephemera; no statement of limitation in the copy). Full essay text is held at assets/sources/archive/excerpts/Tartt-ErinParish-IntoTheBlueAgain-Coploff-1997-catalog.pdf (4 pages).

Nature of the piece: A 15-page personal memoir of Tartt's friendship with William Maxwell (1908–2000), the long-time New Yorker fiction editor and novelist. The essay is one of the most substantive pieces of Tartt nonfiction published between The Secret History (1992) and The Little Friend (2002), and one of only a handful of Tartt prose contributions to a tribute or anthology volume. It is significantly longer than her other contributed pieces (Bomb Magazine "Editor's Choice" §7.7, Artforum "Portfolio" §7.12, Murder For Love poem §7.5).

Content summary: Tartt recounts meeting Maxwell at New York dinner parties when she was a young first novelist. Key episodes documented in the essay:

  • A dinner at which Maxwell inscribed Tartt's copy of So Long, See You Tomorrow: "Dear Donna, I hope you like my farmers. Bill." [Bibliographic note: documentary evidence of a Tartt-association copy of a Maxwell first edition inscribed by Maxwell to Tartt — whereabouts unknown; not in Ole Miss MUM00441 finding aid per §10.3; presumably retained by Tartt.]
  • A dinner party where she and Maxwell sat side-by-side as the oldest and youngest guests, discovering shared roots: Maxwell's Lincoln, Illinois childhood mapped onto Tartt's Greenwood, Mississippi childhood — Illinois Central Railroad running through both towns, traveling salesmen in both families (Maxwell's father, Tartt's grandfather), early loss of a maternal figure (Maxwell lost his mother at ten; Tartt lost a great-grandmother who was "more mother than grandmother" at nine).
  • Maxwell's observation to Tartt: "One carries the mark all one's life. I suppose it's what makes people writers… Look at Virginia Woolf. In an artist's childhood, there's almost always something missing, isn't there?"
  • A New York street encounter Tartt regrets to this day — passing Maxwell without speaking because she didn't want to make him stand in the cold; she never saw him again.
  • Attendance at Maxwell's memorial service, which she describes as "the most cheerful memorial service I've ever attended, with the pony, and the balloons, and the music, and the ice-cream cart, and Emily's drawings displayed in the church, and a peacock belonging to St. John the Divine strutting about aggressively at the feet of the funeral guests." [Locational detail: Cathedral of St. John the Divine, Manhattan.]
  • A self-described post-Maxwell-death episode while "editing my second novel" (i.e., The Little Friend, between 2000 and 2002) — Tartt rose from bed "like a sleepwalker," pulled a Maxwell book from the shelf at random, opened it, and found "your plain sensible answer to my question leapt right out at me." This dates an editing-period anecdote for The Little Friend to 2000–2002 and confirms Tartt was working on TLF revisions during the period immediately after Maxwell's July 2000 death.

The essay closes: "I can hear you talking even now, in your wise, kind voice, though you've been away from the apartment on Eighty-sixth Street for a good while now." [Locational detail: Maxwell's New York residence was on East 86th Street.]

Collector significance:

  • Substantive Tartt nonfiction. At 15 pages, "Mr. Maxwell" is among the longest single Tartt nonfiction pieces ever published — substantially longer than the Harper's "Sleepytown" essay (§5.1 / §6 cross-reference), the Willie Morris tribute "My friend, my mentor, my inspiration" (§6.4), and all of her catalogued magazine contributions. It is comparable in scale to the True Grit introduction (§7.1).
  • Original to the volume. Per the Permissions page, the essay was commissioned for this anthology and has no prior magazine publication. The 2004 first edition is the only print appearance of "Mr. Maxwell" in any format.
  • Biographically dateable detail. The essay provides datable evidence that Tartt was actively editing The Little Friend in the period immediately after July 2000 — directly relevant to the TLF chronology in §3.
  • Maxwell-inscribed So Long, See You Tomorrow implied. Tartt's own copy of So Long, See You Tomorrow with the "Dear Donna, I hope you like my farmers. Bill" inscription is a documented Tartt-association copy of a Maxwell first that has never surfaced in the open dealer market.

Structural outline of Tartt's afterword (9 pages, scanned PDF archived at assets/sources/archive/Tartt-Afterword-FolioSociety-WHALITC-2023-Scribd.pdf):

  • pp. 177–178 (opening): Frames Jackson as a "biting singularity in twentieth-century letters" alongside Sylvia Plath; argues Castle is preoccupied less with the murders than with "the madness of the commonplace mind: its herd instinct, its dark, gloating misogyny, and the old American urge—trailing all the way back to the ducking pool at Salem—to bully and shame and humiliate anyone who is different." Contrasts Jackson's commercial "housewife work" (Good Housekeeping, Women's Home Companion) with her major fiction; argues the same "strain of resignation in the face of anguish" runs through both, expanded in Castle into a parable of "being able to make happiness for oneself anywhere, in the most brutal circumstances."
  • pp. 178–180: Introduces Merricat Blackwood with the novel's famous opening declaration ("My name is Mary Katherine Blackwood. I am eighteen years old…"). Recounts the arsenic-laced sugar bowl backstory and the Constance trial. Connects Constance Blackwood to Lizzie Borden and Emma Borden (acquitted but shunned), and to Florence Maybrick, the heiress poisoner — historical women accused of murdering family members.
  • pp. 180–184 (central analysis): Reads Castle as a study of arrogant feminine power outside conventional morality. Discusses the social-ostracism plot, the mob assault on the Blackwood house, Uncle Julian's role as memory-keeper, and Jackson's habit of casting women "unwilling or unable to play along with the roles demanded of them."
  • p. 185 (closing): Cites P. D. Ouspensky on the artist as clairvoyant and magician; references Jackson's own mischievous claim to have broken Alfred A. Knopf's leg by casting a spell on him; closes with a flourish comparing Jackson and the Blackwood sisters to Plath's Lady Lazarus — "arrogant goddesses rising out of the ash with their red hair, glorying in the crackle of destruction, dangerous and exhilarating to the end."

Notable for §3 / Tartt-as-critic cross-reference: The afterword's framing of Jackson alongside Plath, and the use of Ouspensky's clairvoyant-artist concept, echoes Tartt's broader critical voice — compare the 2024 Martel Reclaiming Art introduction (§7.6), which deploys related "art as magic" framing, and the 2005 Life of St. Francis foreword (§7.23), which similarly treats subject-as-magician.

Tartt's Q&A in the Henry Holt collection — contents:

  • Currently reading: Jean Cocteau's Difficulty of Being (Neversink Library reissue); Rachel Kushner's Telex from Cuba; Gilbert Highet's Poets in a Landscape. Bedside: Byron: The Last Journey by Harold Nicolson; Horse, Flower, Bird by Kate Bernheimer; Barry Paris's biography of Louise Brooks; Rifleman: A Front-Line Life by Victor Gregg with Rick Stroud. Comfort book: Raymond Chandler's The Big Sleep.
  • Best book read this year: Cyril Connolly's The Unquiet Grave (re-read; carried with her in France years ago as one of six books).
  • Favorite novelists (19th C): Dickens, Melville, James, Conrad, Stevenson, Dostoyevsky — Dickens first.
  • Favorite novelists (20th C): Nabokov, Evelyn Waugh, Salinger, Fitzgerald, Don DeLillo.
  • Favorite novelists (21st C): Edward St. Aubyn and Paul Murray.
  • Best part of writing a novel: "I love having an alternate life to retreat into and to lose myself in. I love being away from the world so long—so far out from shore. Eleven years." (Goldfinch's eleven-year composition explicitly invoked.)
  • Hardest part: "Formulat[ing] an answer when someone asks me: What's your book about?"
  • Stories drawn to: not contemporary American realism, marriage, parenting, suburbia, divorce. Cites Nabokov: "all I want from a book is the tingle down the spine, for my hairs to stand on end."
  • Self-help: "I was a great fan of the now defunct Loompanics press, which published such self-help classics as The Complete Guide to Lock Picking and How to Disappear Completely and Never Be Found."
  • Heroes growing up: Huckleberry Finn and Peter Pan (as a child); Franny Glass (as a teenager); Agatha Runcible (in her twenties).
  • Dinner date with a writer (dead or alive): Albert Camus. "That trench coat! That cigarette! I think my French is good enough. We'd have a great time."
  • Disappointing/overrated: "I don't like Hemingway. And I know I don't love Ulysses as much as I am supposed to—but then again, I never cared even one-tenth so much for the Odyssey as I do for the Iliad."
  • Last book put down without finishing: "Definitely I do, but impolitic to say."
  • Character to be from literature: Mrs. Stitch (from Waugh's Scoop) "driving around madly in her tiny motorcar"; or Tom Ripley.
  • Always meant to read but haven't: Shelby Foote's three-volume Civil War history; most of Thomas Hardy's novels ("his novels are just too sad for me").
  • Will read next: Lord Rochester's Monkey by Graham Greene; Doctor Sleep by Stephen King.
  • Author bio in volume: "Donna Tartt is the author of the novels The Goldfinch, The Secret History, and The Little Friend."

Original NYTBR Q&A date: Sunday, October 20, 2013. Sourced from the canonical NYT URL slug /2013/10/20/books/review/donna-tartt-by-the-book.html. The column appeared in the print Book Review of Sunday October 20, 2013 — exactly two days before the U.S. publication of The Goldfinch (October 22, 2013) and five days before Stephen King's full NYTBR cover review of the novel (October 25, 2013). Pamela Paul's editor's introduction in the Henry Holt 2014 collection confirms this sequence: "Donna Tartt told me how much she looked forward to reading Stephen King's new novel—before he'd raved about The Goldfinch on our cover." This makes the By the Book Q&A the first major NYTBR appearance during the Goldfinch launch cycle; Tartt's "will read next" mention of King's Doctor Sleep (published Sept 24, 2013) is consistent with the timing.

Editor's note (Pamela Paul) referencing Tartt: "Donna Tartt told me how much she looked forward to reading Stephen King's new novel—before he'd raved about The Goldfinch on our cover." Also: "Michael Chabon, Jeffrey Eugenides, and Donna Tartt were all consumed by the Patrick Melrose novels of Edward St. Aubyn."
